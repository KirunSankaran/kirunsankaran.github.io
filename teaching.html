Teaching

I teach moral and political philosophy as being about our common life, and I run a classroom on the premise that students with different backgrounds, preparation, and politics should be able to argue with one another — and with me — on equal terms. Three commitments organize my courses. Productive disagreement: students practice defending views they reject, canonical texts are paired with live controversies so disagreement has stakes, and discussion is managed so criticism lands on arguments rather than people — the part of my record I'm proudest of is that students routinely report they can't identify my own politics. Evaluating arguments: nearly everything I assign builds two skills, stating an argument better than its author did and constructing the strongest case for a view. Multidisciplinary reading: my courses pair philosophy with the best empirical work on how the concepts operate in practice, so students stop treating the questions as abstractions.
